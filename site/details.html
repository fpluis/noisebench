How the run worked

Two ways of asking, over one set of questions.

Direct
How likely is this?
Each market put to each model as a probability request, four times for its Yes outcome and four times for its No outcome. The question, the rules, the dates and the research are identical between the two; only which of the market's two outcomes is being asked for changes. A coherent forecaster's two answers add to 100%.
Head-to-head
Which is likelier?
Fifty fixed pairs of markets, ranked against each other with no probability attached, in all four combinations of requested outcome, twice each. Flipping the requested outcome on both sides must reverse the answer.

The pairs are fixed, not random. They were built by sorting the questions by market price and pairing neighbours, so the two sides are deliberately close in likelihood and the comparison is hard. Asking for the market's own No outcome rather than rewriting the question into a negated one is deliberate: a hand-negated question contradicts rules that are still written for Yes, which measures the contradiction rather than the model.

The five measures

Each is a failure rate with a genuine zero, and each is reported as a plain percentage — but of two different things, which the unit column spells out. Two are absolute distances in probability: a 3.0% average error means a model's repeated answers to one question sat 3 points of probability apart, not that they varied by 3% of their own size. Three are shares of judgments: a 12.0% pairwise disagreement means 12 of every 100 comparable judgments came back different.

Why the four wordings must disagree

Each pair is asked four ways. Those four answers only probe two independent facts, and each fact gets asked twice in opposite directions — which is what makes a coherence check possible without knowing any true answer.

Questions that went unanswered

Not every request came back with a usable number. Those attempts are absent from the analysis rather than counted as noise. A repeat set holding fewer than two surviving forecasts is skipped by average error instead of scoring zero, and a question answered in only one wording is skipped by negation error.

Reproducing this

The site reads static JSON only, so once the analysis has run it needs no database.

The five measures live in src/metrics.ts, are unit-tested in tests/metrics.test.ts — including a worked example of each — and are emitted to site/data/metrics.json, which with run.json is everything these pages read. The analysis layer also still writes noise.json, a fuller variance decomposition of the same forecasts on both the probability and log-odds scales, and negation.json; no page reads them.
