What was measured

Every number on this site is about a model's agreement with itself. None of it scores a forecast against how a market actually resolved — the questions were still open when they were asked, and resolution is outside what this benchmark tries to say. Where a market price appears, it describes what the crowd thought at the moment the question was put to the models, and is used only as context for grouping questions.

How the run worked

Two ways of asking, over one set of questions.

Direct
How likely is this?
Each question put to each model as a probability request, in the market's own wording and in its negation, four times each. The two wordings should produce answers that add to 100%.
Head-to-head
Which is likelier?
Fifty fixed pairs of questions, ranked against each other with no probability attached, in all four wording combinations, twice each. Negating both sides must reverse the answer.

The pairs are fixed, not random. They were built by sorting the questions by market price and pairing neighbours, so the two sides are deliberately close in likelihood and the comparison is hard.

The noise vocabulary

The three-way split comes from Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein's Noise, which decomposes the disagreement in a panel of judges. This site uses plainer names for the same quantities. The mapping:

Units

Noise figures are shown two ways, and the choice matters more than it looks. Most of these questions are long shots — half sit below a 20% market price — and the two units disagree about what "noisy" means down there.

Percentage points
14.6 pts
How far the answer moves in absolute terms. A forecast wandering between 50% and 53% moves 3 points; one wandering between 1% and 3% moves 2 points, and looks quieter.
Odds multiple
×3.2
How far it moves relative to how rare the event is. That same 1% to 3% wander is a tripling of the odds — a far bigger claim than the 3-point move, and only this unit registers it.

Neither is the "right" one, which is why every noise chart offers both. The odds multiple is computed in log-odds and reported as e^SD; forecasts are clipped into the 0.1%–99.9% range first, so a single 0.01% answer cannot dominate a whole cell.

The consistency score

Five checks, equally weighted, each a plain share of something countable. They do not share a floor: a model answering at random would score 0% on the first, about 67% on the second and 50% on the rest. Rather than rescale them into something less legible, the charts draw that combined baseline — — — as a line.

Why the four wordings must disagree

Each pair is asked four ways. Those four answers only probe two independent facts, and each fact gets asked twice in opposite directions — which is what makes a coherence check possible without knowing any true answer.

Questions that went unanswered

Not every request came back with a usable number. Those attempts are absent from the analysis rather than counted as noise — but they are not spread evenly across models, so the noise figures are also reported on the subset of questions every model completed in full.

Reproducing this

The site reads static JSON only, so once the analysis has run it needs no database.
